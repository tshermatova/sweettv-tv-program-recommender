# Section 8 — Which recommendation strategy would you select for this platform, and why?

## The two users, method by method

Both users have a history (no test user is cold-start by the log's own definition), but
they sit at opposite ends of evidence volume: HEAVY has 3,733 events over 445 distinct
programmes and a pool of 450 candidates; LIGHT has 1 event, 1 distinct programme, and a
pool of 81. Both have exactly five relevant programmes in the hold-out window, matching
the "exactly five, most-frequent" target definition.

| method | HEAVY hits | LIGHT hits |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| popularity (baseline) | 1/5 | 3/5 |
| association rules | 1/5 | 3/5 |
| PageRank (PPR) | 1/5 | 3/5 |
| content-based (CBF) | 1/5 | 0/5 |
| matrix factorization (iALS) | 0/5 | 0/5 |
| **FINAL — LambdaRank, 41 features** | **1/5** | **3/5** |

For HEAVY, five of six methods (popularity, association rules, PageRank, CBF, the final
ranker) land the same single hit, id 240081 ("Факты. День"), and otherwise fill their
lists with the user's own top-watched informational shows (id 2400480, 2400508,
12001682, 6500479) — items the user watches often but that are not among the five the
target definition actually selects for this window. These lists are familiar, not
discoveries: they reflect habitual channel-surfing, and for four of five slots that
habit does not line up with the specific five "most frequent at ≥0.8 screen time"
programmes the metric rewards. iALS diverges completely, surfacing serials and
entertainment titles (id 10003014, 300358, 1000589, 2500430, 400426) absent from the
user's own top-six watched list, scoring 0/5 — its latent factors pull toward a
different part of this user's broad 445-programme taste space than the frequency-based
methods do.

For LIGHT, the single watched item (id 90071788, a film) carries almost no information,
so every method falls back on population-level signal. Popularity, association rules and
PPR converge on the same three hits (240081, 2400467, 2400508) — recurring news/info
programmes that are globally common and, evidently, also what this nearly-historyless
user ends up watching. PPR's only difference from popularity is that it spends its top
rank on the user's single watched film (a miss) before converging to the same three hits
— personalizing the top of the list with the one piece of evidence available, at the
cost of a slot. CBF and iALS both miss entirely (0/5): built only from that one film,
they recommend more films and serials by content/embedding similarity, none matching the
five generic info programmes actually relevant. The final ranker matches popularity's
3/5.

## How the methods differ

**Association rules vs. matrix factorization.** For HEAVY, association rules reproduce
the user's own frequency ranking almost exactly, because co-occurrence counts are a
direct read of repeat-watch habit. iALS does not: it reconstructs a broader latent taste
space from the whole interaction matrix and places this 445-programme history nearer
unrelated serials and entertainment titles than their actual daily-watch news habit.
Association rules capture literal repetition; matrix factorization captures a smoothed
"similar taste" that, for a heavy, diverse viewer, can drift from the specific habitual
pattern the target rewards.

**Content attributes where co-view signal is thin.** LIGHT has only one interaction, so
CBF extrapolates from that single film's content attributes (genre, actors, director)
and recommends more films and serials. The five relevant programmes for this user are
generic daily info shows with no content overlap with a film, so CBF scores 0/5:
content similarity needs a content-relevant history, and one unrelated item is enough
to mislead it entirely.

**PageRank's graph diffusion.** For HEAVY, PPR's list is identical to association rules'
— with 3,733 history events feeding a well-connected neighbourhood, the random walk has
converged to the same ranking that raw co-occurrence counts give. For LIGHT, by contrast,
diffusion visibly personalizes: the walk starts at the user's one watched item and ranks
it first, ahead of the global top show. Graph diffusion adds the most where personal
evidence is thin (it still injects what little there is at the top of the list) and
reduces to co-occurrence counts once evidence is abundant.

**Where popularity already does well.** For LIGHT, the popularity baseline alone gets
3/5 — equal to the final model — because the relevant programmes are mainstream,
always-on informational shows that dominate the viewing population regardless of
individual history. The same shows also appear among HEAVY's own top-watched list,
which is why nearly every method converges on them for both users. Popularity is already
a strong, nearly-free baseline whenever the relevant items are the generically popular
ones; its measured hold-out score (0.27387) under the true target is the floor against
which every other method must earn its keep.

## Why LambdaRank is the strategy to ship

The shipped 41-feature LambdaRanker scores 0.43949 ± 0.00095 on the calibrated hold-out
(true target, 3 seeds) against 0.27387 for popularity and 0.37324 for own-repeats
weighted by airings — a clear, reproducible margin over both naive baselines. On
Kaggle, the equivalent submission (sub25_top5_s0) scores private 0.44205, ahead of the
frozen leaderboard's own first place (private 0.42912). That comparison is on the
competition's real private score, not an internal estimate.

Alternatives that add computational cost were tested and rejected on direct measurement,
not assumption. A six-way LightGBM rank fusion scored private 0.43419–0.43422 and a
LightGBM+CatBoost fusion scored 0.43405 — both at or below the single-model result,
because the models agree on 5.86 of 5 items on average (39.8% of users get an identical
set from all six variants, 72.6% of items appear in all six lists): there is no
diversity left to fuse, so serving multiple models buys nothing. Cascade re-ranking the
top 20 cost −0.0064 on hold-out, and DART training cost 11.6x the runtime of GBDT for a
loss of −0.0065. Expanding trees/leaves beyond the shipped configuration (2400 trees,
800 trees, num_leaves 63/15) moved the hold-out score by amounts at or below the
seed-to-seed noise floor (0.00178) and were never confirmed on Kaggle. On the hardware
this runs on (an 8 GB, fanless machine supporting one heavy LightGBM job plus one light
job at a time), the shipped 1,600-tree ranker is the affordable ceiling; none of the
heavier alternatives earned their cost.

For the user experience, the two worked examples show what shipping this means in
practice: a "recommended" row from this ranker is dominated by the viewer's own
habitual, highly-rewatched programming (HEAVY) or the platform's generic popular shows
(LIGHT), not by novel content — a safe, familiarity-driven list rather than a
discovery engine, supported directly by the hit-rate numbers above.

## Limitations and failure cases

The pipeline's recommendations are bounded before ranking even starts: only 10.8% of
schedule rows carry a `tv_show_id`, so the remaining 89.2% of broadcast airtime cannot
become a candidate under any method, including the one shipped. Within the labelled
remainder, the candidate pool (139 per user) reaches recall 0.8181 against the true
target — 18.2% of the programmes in a user's actual top-5-most-frequent set are never
offered to any ranking method, and part of that miss is premieres, which by definition
have no broadcast history to generate from. A further 10.79% of candidates never air at
all in the target weeks, so their relevance is necessarily zero regardless of scoring.
The gap between the pool's own ceiling (0.8181) and the shipped model's score (0.43949)
shows most of the unrealized score sits in ranking, not generation — consistent with
the ensembling and ablation results above, where added model capacity is already
exhausted. The test population is also not representative of the full subscriber base
— median 196 history events against 125 for the broader active population — so these
numbers describe an engaged subgroup, not all viewers. Finally, the public/private split
is systematically easier on the public half for every one of the 11 models measured
(+0.0035 to +0.0242), including the frozen leaderboard's own leader, so small
differences anywhere in this report below roughly 0.003 should not be read as settled.

Two qualifications apply throughout. An id a method surfaces that the user did not
watch — iALS's serials for HEAVY, CBF's films for LIGHT — is not evidence of a missed
"discovery"; nothing measured here indicates whether the user would have liked it, only
that it does not match the organisers' top-5 definition of relevant. And the absence of
any programme but "Призрак" from LIGHT's history does not mean this user never watched
anything else: the log records only this platform's own viewing. No online experiment,
A/B test, or revenue effect is claimed anywhere in this section.
