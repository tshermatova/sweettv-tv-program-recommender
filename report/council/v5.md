## 8. Which recommendation strategy, and why

### 8.1 What was tested

Six candidate strategies were run against the same two test users, the organisers' true
top-5 target, and the same candidate pool: a global popularity baseline, association
rules, personalised PageRank (PPR), content-based filtering (CBF), matrix factorisation
(iALS), and the shipped LambdaRank ranker over 41 features. Beyond these two users, the
same question — which strategy to run in production — was tested at scale: 23 rejected
directions (4 that won on the hold-out and then failed on Kaggle, 14 rejected on
validation alone, 2 rejected by direct measurement, 3 rejected by submission) against the
2 accepted changes that produced the shipped model. The two users are not a random pair:
HEAVY (3733 events, 445 distinct programmes, pool of 450) and LIGHT (1 event, 1 distinct
programme, pool of 81) are the extremes of evidence volume among the 1118 of 1260 test
users who have both a history and something to predict against. Neither is a cold-start
case — all 1260 test users appear in the view log — so the contrast is purely about how
much evidence each method has to work with.

| method | HEAVY (5 relevant) | LIGHT (5 relevant) |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| popularity | 1/5 | 3/5 |
| association rules | 1/5 | 3/5 |
| PageRank (PPR) | 1/5 | 3/5 |
| content-based (CBF) | 1/5 | 0/5 |
| matrix factorisation (iALS) | 0/5 | 0/5 |
| **final: LambdaRank, 41 features** | **1/5** | **3/5** |

### 8.2 Familiar preference or new discovery

Every hit in both lists is a programme the user, or users in general, already watch
heavily. HEAVY's single hit for every method bar CBF is `id=240081` (Факты. День), a daily
news programme that also appears 222 times in that user's own training-window history;
the final ranker's only improvement over popularity is to move that item to rank 1 instead
of rank 2. LIGHT's three hits (`240081`, `2400508`, `2400467`) are the platform's three
most-watched informational dailies, not anything inferred from the user's one logged film.
None of the six lists for either user surfaces a correct answer that is not already a
population-level habit. The lists CBF and iALS propose that are *not* hits — serials and
films for LIGHT, niche genre titles for HEAVY — are the only candidates that could be
called "new," and none of them hit. That is consistent with the data but does not prove
the broader point either way: an unfamiliar item is not automatically a useful discovery,
it may simply be wrong, and nothing in this exercise measures whether a user would have
enjoyed any of the misses.

### 8.3 Why the methods differ

For HEAVY, popularity, association rules and PPR produce the *identical* top-5. With 3733
events this user's co-view graph and the population's popularity ranking have converged on
the same answer, so AR's pairwise co-occurrence and PPR's graph diffusion add nothing
beyond what raw frequency already says. CBF diverges because it reasons from
genre/category/director/actor metadata rather than co-viewing, substituting two "Инфо"
programmes the user does not actually watch (`2500261`, `5900347`) for two the user does;
it still lands one hit, but for the wrong structural reason. iALS fails completely (0/5)
for HEAVY despite the richest history of the two users, surfacing serials and variety
shows unrelated to this user's information-heavy habit — its latent factors do not
describe this user's behaviour at the resolution a top-5 list needs.

For LIGHT, with one logged film, the picture is different. Popularity and association
rules still coincide almost exactly, because AR with one history item collapses toward the
same population-level answer popularity already gives. PPR is the one method that visibly
uses the user's own evidence: with only one node to diffuse from, it promotes
`id=90071788` (the one film actually watched) to rank 1 — which is not one of the five
relevant answers, so it costs PPR nothing here but shows graph diffusion doing something
distinct from frequency counting. CBF is where the brief's "content attributes contribute
where co-view signal is thin" claim is tested directly, and it fails: with exactly one
history row, content similarity has only one profile to extend, and it extends it toward
other films and serials, 0/5. Thin co-view signal does not automatically make content
features useful; here the one available content signal pointed away from the five correct
answers, which were informational dailies unrelated by genre to the one film watched. iALS
again scores 0/5. The popularity baseline already does as well as the final ranker on this
user (3/5 each) — the case where popularity is adequate is exactly the thin-history case,
consistent with the aggregate hold-out gap between popularity (0.27387) and the shipped
model (0.43949) being driven by users with more evidence to rank, not by users like LIGHT.

### 8.4 Why LambdaRank over 41 features is the selected strategy

Validation and Kaggle agree on this choice to within the limits the brief establishes.
The shipped configuration — FEATS3's 11 added habit-shape columns, label aligned to the
organisers' top-5 rule, 1600 trees at lr 0.015 — scores 0.43949 ± 0.00095 on the calibrated
hold-out, against 0.27387 for popularity and 0.37324 for own-repeats weighted by airings,
and the designated submission `sub25_top5_s0` scores public 0.45440 / private 0.44205,
above the frozen leaderboard's own first place (public 0.44297 / private 0.42912). The two
components of that configuration are the only two changes in the entire search that
survived Kaggle submission with a positive private delta: FEATS3 (+0.0063 private) and
1600 trees (+0.0008 private).

Everything else that beat it on the hold-out alone did not survive contact with Kaggle,
and that pattern is itself the strongest reason to prefer this simple, confirmed
configuration over anything fancier. FEATS4, FEATS5, FEATS6 and FEATS5+FEATS6 all won on
hold-out A (+0.0027 to +0.0059) and then scored zero or negative on private (0.0000,
−0.0010, −0.0004, −0.0003). Graded labels and airing-restricted candidates read as high
as ~0.465 on the hold-out, but that territory shares its mechanism with the leaky
expected-frequency feature, which read +0.0272 on hold-out and −0.011 on Kaggle private —
the same shape of false win. Ensembling does not help either: six LightGBM variants share
5.86 of 5 items on average (nothing to fuse) and their rank fusion scored private
0.43419/0.43422, below the single model; the more genuinely different LightGBM+CatBoost
fusion still scored 0.43405. Cascade re-ranking the top 20 cost −0.0064. On a MacBook Air
M1 with 8 GB and no fan, where the stated limit is one heavy LightGBM job plus one light
job at a time, none of these were cheap to run either: DART alone costs 11.6x GBDT's
training time for a loss of −0.0065, and a multi-model ensemble multiplies that cost for a
result that, on Kaggle, is worse than the single ranker it would replace.

The lists in §8.2 show what choosing this strategy means for the user experience: for
HEAVY it reorders an already-correct habitual answer; for LIGHT it reproduces the same
population-level top items a much cheaper popularity list would give. Nothing here
licenses a claim about satisfaction, engagement or retention — no online experiment was
run and none is claimed — but the recommendation is accurately described as a *ranking of
known habits*, not a discovery engine.

### 8.5 Limitations the results actually support

The ceiling on all six methods is set before any of them runs: only 10.8% of schedule rows
carry a `tv_show_id`, so 89.2% of broadcast airtime cannot become a candidate item under
any strategy tested. Within the 10.8% that can, the candidate pool (139 items/user) covers
only 81.81% of the true relevant items; 17.1% of correct answers never enter the pool at
all, and 175 of those are premieres — programmes with no prior airing to generate a
candidate from, a failure of candidate generation, not of ranking, that is close to twice
as costly under the corrected target as it looked under the original, miscalibrated one.
iALS failed on both users shown here, one data-rich and one data-poor; two users do not
prove iALS fails generally, only that it failed on the two cases examined. The LIGHT
user's one logged event establishes only that this platform recorded one view; it does
not establish the user "never" watched anything else, on this or another platform, and
"popularity already does well" is true of this one thin-history user under this metric,
not a general claim that content or graph methods are unnecessary elsewhere.
