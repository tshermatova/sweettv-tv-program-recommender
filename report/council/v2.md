## 8. Which recommendation strategy would you select for this platform, and why?

**What this section cannot show.** Two users cannot support a general claim about method
quality, and the evidence below should be read that way. Nothing here demonstrates that a
user enjoyed, disliked, or even watched to completion any programme in any list — the
hold-out only checks membership in a set of five frequent airings. No A/B test, online
experiment, or revenue effect is claimed, and none follows from a static hold-out. The
labelled universe itself is narrow: only 10.8% of schedule rows carry a `tv_show_id`, so
every method compared here, including the one recommended, is choosing among the same
minority of catalogued airtime and is blind to the rest by construction, not by a modelling
choice that could be tuned away.

**The two users, hit by hit.** The HEAVY user (3 733 events, 445 programmes, pool 450) has
5 relevant programmes in the window. Popularity, association rules, and personalised
PageRank return the *identical* five-item list in the *identical* order — one hit
(`240081`, Факты. День) at rank 2. Content-based filtering (CBF) returns a different list,
also one hit, at rank 4. Matrix factorisation (iALS) returns five items that share nothing
with the relevant set: zero hits. The final LambdaRank model also scores one hit, but moves
it to rank 1. By the metric's own definition (denominator `min(5,|relevant|)`), a single hit
at rank 1 contributes `(1/1)/5 = 0.20` to that user's AP; the same hit at rank 2 contributes
`(1/2)/5 = 0.10`. So on this one user, the ranker's entire measured advantage over
popularity/AR/PPR is a reordering, not a new correct item, and it is worth exactly twice the
per-user AP — illustrative arithmetic, not a claim about the aggregate gap.

The LIGHT user (1 event, 1 programme, pool 81) also has 5 relevant programmes. Popularity and
association rules again return the identical list and the identical 3/5 hits. PageRank
returns the same three hits, reordered, with the user's one historical item pulled to rank 1
(a miss). CBF and iALS, the two methods that lean hardest on this user's single data point,
both score zero. The final model matches the 3/5 hit count of popularity/AR, trading one
miss (`6500479`) for a different miss (`300358`). On neither example does the shipped model
recommend a correct item that popularity misses.

**Do the lists reflect familiar preferences or offer something new?** For HEAVY, four of the
final model's five candidates (`240081`, `2400480`, `2400508`, `12001682`) are already in this
user's own most-watched training-window list; only `6500479` is not. The model's practical
behaviour here is re-serving known habits, not discovery. For LIGHT, with only one prior
event, there is almost no personal history to re-serve, and the final list is effectively
drawn from population-level popular programmes — generic by necessity, not personalised. An
unfamiliar item on either list (iALS's films for HEAVY, CBF's and iALS's films/series for
LIGHT) is not shown to be a good or bad recommendation by this data; it is only shown to be
absent from the five programmes the organisers' rule counted as relevant for that user. And
the LIGHT user's single logged event does not establish that this person is new to
television, or to these programmes — only that this platform recorded one view before the
training cutoff.

**Why the methods differ.** Association rules encode direct co-occurrence counts with no
compression; where the signal is "these items are watched together," as with HEAVY's dense,
information-bulletin-heavy history, AR reduces to the same ranking as raw popularity, because
the globally popular items *are* this user's frequent co-occurrences. Matrix factorisation
compresses the interaction matrix into latent factors to generalise across many users at
once; on both example users that compression produced a candidate set sharing nothing with
the relevant five. Two users cannot establish that iALS is broadly weaker, but the result is
at least consistent with the aggregate finding that adding collaborative richness never
helped here (an extended 9-signal collaborative block cost −0.001 on validation; a
recent-window collaborative block, FEATS7, cost −0.0040 — both pre-correction hold-out
figures, so uncertain in scale but directionally the only evidence available). Content-based
filtering contributes candidates from genre and category, not necessarily relevant ones: for
LIGHT, the one logged event is a film, so CBF proposes more films and series, while the
user's actual relevant five appear to be the same informational programmes that recur across
both examples — a category mismatch CBF cannot see past with a single data point. PageRank's
graph diffusion reshapes the *order* around a seed node (pulling LIGHT's own watched item to
rank 1) without changing the candidate set measurably: the brief's own instrumentation found
PageRank's top-20 adds zero new candidates to the pool beyond what AR and popularity already
surface. Popularity already does well — 1/5 and 3/5 on these two users, matching three other
methods outright — because informational/news programming saturates the relevant set for a
heavy multi-category viewer and a near-historyless viewer alike.

**Why the shipped strategy is still preferable.** Not because it beats every baseline on
these two users — it does not, by hit count. It is preferable because its gain is confirmed
on two independent measurements at the corrected scale: hold-out MAP@5 0.43949 ± 0.00095
against 0.27387 for popularity and 0.37324 for own-repeats, and a submitted Kaggle private
score of 0.44205 (`sub25_top5_s0`), above the frozen leaderboard's own first place at
0.42912. At this hardware scale — a fanless M1 MacBook Air with 8 GB, one heavy LightGBM job
at a time — the alternatives that spend more compute are the ones that were tested and
failed: a six-model rank fusion (private 0.43419/0.43422, five of six models sharing 5.86 of
5 items — nothing to fuse), a LightGBM+CatBoost fusion (private 0.43405, despite being the
most genuinely different pairing tried), and a 20-item cascade re-ranker (hold-out 0.43315,
−0.0064). None of this machinery earned its cost. What the two-user traces show about user
experience is an honest description of what the chosen model actually does: for an engaged
viewer it mostly re-ranks that viewer's own repeats toward the top of the list; for a
near-historyless viewer it falls back toward population habits. That matches the pipeline as
built — candidates are a user's own watched items plus the 80 globally most popular — and is
consistent with own-repeats alone reaching 0.37324 of the 0.43949 ceiling the full model
reaches. The strategy is a refinement of familiarity and popularity, not a discovery engine,
and should be described to a reader of this report as exactly that.

**Limitations and failure cases the results actually support.** Start with the 10.8% figure:
most of what airs on this platform is structurally invisible to every method compared,
including the chosen one. Within the labelled 10.8%, the candidate pool (139/user) has true
recall 0.8181 under the corrected target — identical to the MAP@5 ceiling, because with
exactly five relevant items per user (true for 90.9% of them) recall and ceiling coincide; 18.19%
of relevant items are structurally unreachable by any ranking. 17.1% of correct answers are
not candidates at all, and 175 of those are premieres — items with no prior airing history,
which AR, PageRank, and iALS cannot reach by construction. 1.9% of users have no correct
answer in the pool whatsoever; no ranking fixes that for them. Feature-level ablations (e.g.
leave-one-out importance for `ch_air_aff` and `frac_mean`) and most of the hyperparameter
sweep were measured on the pre-correction hold-out and are explicitly uncertain again at the
true scale; only results confirmed by a Kaggle submission — FEATS4/5/6, the 0.50 label, the
switching rule, both rank fusions, and pool 160 — survive the correction with confidence.
Finally, the public/private gap is positive for every one of 11 models measured, including
the leaderboard leader's, so any single public number (this model's own 0.45440, or the
external repository's reported 0.45497) systematically overstates what the private score will
show, and two classmates' reported private scores of 0.437 and 0.45 remain unexplained by
anything measured in this project.
